Probability and Statistics

Random Variable

A random variable (r.v) XXX is a measurable function from a probability space (Ω,A,P)(\Omega , \mathcal A, \mathbb P)(Ω,A,P) to a measurable space (E,E)(E, \mathcal E)(E,E)

If you don’t understand anything, that’s okay. You just need to remember that this is an application of a large space we don’t know about to our real world. If you want to fully understand this definition, take a look at the Appendix.

Law of X

The law of XXX or the probability distribution of XXX is its measure of probability PX\mathbb P_XPX​ on (E,E)(E, \mathcal E)(E,E). ∀ε∈E,PX:=(X−1(ε))\forall \varepsilon \in \mathcal E, \mathbb P_X := (X^{-1}(\varepsilon))∀ε∈E,PX​:=(X−1(ε))

If you don’t understand anything again, that’s okay too. You just need to remember that the law of XXX is a function that describes how the values of XXX are distributed. Many of these are already known and have names: bernoulli, normal, gamma, etc. A part of the job of a probability/statistics researcher is to play with them. Statistical models

Statistical modeling is the basis of all statistical inference. To model an experiment is to propose a theoretical law for the random variable X=(X1,...,Xn)X = (X_1, . . . , X_n)X=(X1​,...,Xn​).

Statistical model

A statistical model M\mathcal MM is the tuple M:=(Xn,An,P)\mathcal M := ( \mathcal X^n, \mathcal A^n, \mathcal P)M:=(Xn,An,P) where (Xn,An)(\mathcal X^n, \mathcal A^n)(Xn,An) is a measurable space, P\mathcal PP is a family of probability law (i.e P=(Pθn)θ∈Θ\mathcal P = (\mathbb P^n_\theta)_{\theta \in \Theta}P=(Pθn​)θ∈Θ​) and nnn is just the number of variable.

It may seem complicated at first, but reassuringly it’s not. Let’s look at an example through an exercise.

Exercise

Give the statistical model of a mmm identical flipping coin. A priori, we don’t know if the coins are balanced.

The number of coin is mmm so we have n=mn=mn=m. Note: Maybe you’re frustrated because you haven’t found Bernouilli’s law, that’s normal. In statistical modeling, the family of laws often comes from the hat like that. If the distribution is i.i.d, we can use the direct notation M\mathcal MM is the tuple M:=(X,A,Pθ∈Θ)\mathcal M := ( \mathcal X, \mathcal A, \mathbb P_{\theta\in \Theta})M:=(X,A,Pθ∈Θ​)

Parametric model

M\mathcal MM is said parametric when Θ\ThetaΘ has a finite dimension.

Unparametric model

M\mathcal MM is said unparametric when Θ\ThetaΘ has a unfinished dimension.

As unparametric is less restrictive than parametric, it can be used with a wide range of assumptions.

Identifiability

M\mathcal MM is identifiable when ∀θ,θ′∈Θ,Pθ=Pθ′⇒θ=θ′\forall \theta, \theta ' \in \Theta, \mathbb P_\theta = \mathbb P_{\theta'} \Rightarrow \theta = \theta'∀θ,θ′∈Θ,Pθ​=Pθ′​⇒θ=θ′

This assumption is essential for a good machine learning model and is often forgotten by data scientists. I hope the example below speaks for itself.

It’s important to understand what likelihood is, and how it differs from classical probability. It’s the same function, but not from the same point of view. For probabilities, we know the framework in which we’re doing the experiment, for example, I know that my coin is fair and I can calculate the probabilities from there (the density is a function of xxx, the expected result). In the case of likelihood, we don’t know the precise framework, but knowing the results, we try to assign a probability of having these results for each possible framework (the density is a function of θ\thetaθ, the setting parameter).
